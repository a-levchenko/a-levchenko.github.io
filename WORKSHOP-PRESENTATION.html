A workshop walkthrough

You describe the job.
It gets built.

This is a guided tour of what happens between the sentence you type and working Salesforce functionality — tested, reviewed, documented and ready to hand over.

You will not be writing code. You will not be using a terminal. You will be answering questions about your own business, and approving a plan.

Two moments are yours

Everything in this colour is a point where the work stops and waits for you. There are only two of them, and together they take about fifteen minutes.

Everything else runs itself

Investigation, design, criticism of that design, building, testing, deployment, code review and paperwork. You can close the laptop lid.

Tip: use ← and → on your keyboard to move through this deck.

Who is actually doing the work

Five specialists, one conversation

Behind the single chat window there is a small team. Each member does one job and hands on to the next. Only one of them ever talks to you.

O

The orchestrator talks to you

Your business analyst. Asks the questions, runs the whole process, writes the paperwork, saves everything. The one you type to.

A

The architect

Investigates how your Salesforce is set up today, then designs the solution and decides the technical approach.

C

The critic

Attacks the design looking for holes and things that cannot actually be built. It is never allowed to suggest its own idea — only to find problems.

D

The developer

Builds the approved design. Writes the tests first, then the thing that makes them pass.

R

The reviewer

Checks the finished work with fresh eyes, knowing nothing about why the developer did what it did. Finds problems, ranks them, sends the serious ones back.

Why split them up? The same mind that designs something is poor at spotting its own blind spots. Separating the roles is the point, not an accident.

Step 2 of 6 · your turn

It interviews you

Questions arrive as a short list of choices, with the suggested answer first. There is always an "Other" if none of them fit. Try answering these three.

What you will be asked about

  • Who uses it, and in what role
  • What they see and do, step by step
  • What must never happen
  • Whether mistakes can be undone, and who is accountable
  • How often it is used, and how much data
  • What you explicitly do not want
  • How you will check it works
What you will never be asked: anything technical. Not which Salesforce feature to use, not what to name things, not how to build it. Those are the agent's decisions — it makes them, writes down why, and shows you a one-line summary you may overrule.

What you get
A register of every item, a one-click hand-out and return screen, and an overdue list — all inside Salesforce, reachable from its own menu entry.

How it works
Reception picks a person and an available item, sets a due date, and hands it over. The item immediately shows as out. On return they note its condition and it becomes available again.

What it will not do
No e-mails, no reminders. No employee access. No extending a loan. No history of who asked for something — only what was actually lent.

Risks
If reception forgets to record a return, the item stays "out" until someone corrects it by hand.

Size
Small. About a day of building.

Technical detail

Components (7) · Equipment__c — object, 5 fields · Loan__c — object, 7 fields + Overdue formula · LoanTriggerHandler — refuses double lending · equipmentHandout — the screen · Equipment Reception — permission set · Equipment Loans — app + 2 tabs · 3 list views Decisions: the double-lending check is code rather than a configuration rule, because it must compare across records. Overdue is a calculated field, so nothing runs on a timer.

Your paperwork

Everything it writes, and where it lands

One folder per task, named after your reference number. Nothing is ever written anywhere else. Click a folder.

Why so much paperwork? Because the next person — an administrator, a colleague, you in six months — needs to know not just what was built but what you asked for and why it was built that way. All of it travels with the work.

After it finishes

What you do with the result

The agent stops at your practice system. It never touches the live one, and it never decides that work is ready to release. That part is yours.

1 · Try it yourselfOpen your practice Salesforce and walk through the thing you asked for, once. Five minutes of clicking tells you more than any report.
2 · Read the two guidesThe user guide should describe something you recognise. If it does not, something was misunderstood — say so.
3 · Hand it to your developersThey review the actual changes and put it through your normal release process. The installation checklist tells them exactly what to move and what to do by hand.
4 · Decide on the small stuffMinor improvements the reviewer suggested but did not make are listed. Raise them as new tasks or let them go.
5 · Keep the folderIt is the record of what you asked for and what was agreed. It answers the "why is it like this?" question six months from now.
Your normal release checks still apply. A clean run by the agent does not replace your team's own gates — it gets the work to that gate in good shape.

Translation

Words you will see, in plain English

You do not need to use any of these. You will see them scroll past, and it helps to know they are ordinary things with intimidating names.

Org

One Salesforce system. Your company has several: a live one that the business runs on, and practice copies for building things. The agent only ever works in a practice one.

Sandbox / practice org

A separate copy of Salesforce for trying things. Breaking it affects nobody.

Production

The live system. The agent refuses to touch it. There is no setting to override that.

Deploy

Installing the finished work into a Salesforce system so it actually exists there and can be used.

Metadata

The shape of your Salesforce — the fields, screens, rules and permissions. As opposed to the data, which is the actual records.

Object / field

An object is a kind of thing you store — an item of equipment, a loan. A field is one piece of information about it — its asset tag, its due date.

Apex

Salesforce's programming language. Used when a rule is too complex to configure by pointing and clicking — for example, checking whether an item is already out.

Flow

Automation you build by configuring rather than programming. Preferred where it is enough, because it is easier for an administrator to change later.

LWC

Lightning Web Component — a custom screen or part of a screen, when the standard Salesforce layouts will not do what you need.

Permission set

A bundle of access rights you grant to particular people. "Reception staff can use the loan screen" is a permission set.

Git · branch · commit · push

The system that keeps every version of the work. A branch is your task's own workspace, a commit is a labelled snapshot, and push shares it with your team. The agent does all of this itself.

Pull request

Formally proposing that your task's work be merged into the team's main version. A human opens it, reviews it, and accepts it.

Test coverage

How much of the new code is exercised by automated tests. Salesforce refuses to accept work into the live system below a set threshold.

Static analysis

An automatic inspection for known bad patterns — security holes, inefficiency — without running anything. It runs on every file the agent writes.

Don't panic

When it stops and asks you something

The agent is built to stop rather than guess. Every stop arrives as a specific question with options, not an error message. Here are the ones you will actually meet.

"Which Salesforce should I use?"Name your practice system. If you accidentally name the live one, it stops dead and will not continue.
"The document you mentioned isn't there"Check the file path, or tell it to carry on without the document.
"I found a question I can't answer"A business decision surfaced during design. Answer it the same way as the interview.
"Your Salesforce and your team's records disagree"Someone changed something directly in Salesforce that is not in the team's version. It will not guess which one is right — tell it.
"This would delete something"Anything irreversible needs you to say yes explicitly. Read it carefully, then decide.
"The reviewer and the developer disagree"Rare. You get both positions in plain language and make the call.

To carry on

Just reply in the same chat. It picks up exactly where it stopped.

If you closed everything

Reopen and type the same command with just your task reference. It finds the folder, works out how far it got, and continues.

Take these away

Five things worth remembering

1

Spend your attention on the interview and the approval

They are short, and they are the only two places where your judgement cannot be substituted. Everything else is machinery.

2

Always read "what it will not do"

That paragraph is the whole negotiation. It is where the plan stays small — and where something you needed might be missing.

3

Ask for the smallest thing that works

Let it add later. Removing something already built is far more expensive than adding to something simple.

4

One request, one task, one folder

Do not pile a second idea into a running task. Start a new one — they are cheap.

5

Never ask it to skip a check

If something is blocked, there is a reason worth understanding. It is built to refuse, and that is a feature you are paying for.

That is the whole thing

You describe the job in your own words, answer some questions about your business, approve a short plan, and come back to working, tested, documented Salesforce. Everything else is someone else's problem — and that someone else does not get tired, skip the tests, or forget to write the guide.
